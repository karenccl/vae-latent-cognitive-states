# Recovering Latent Cognitive States from Choice and Reaction-Time Sequences: A Simulation Study Comparing a Variational Autoencoder with Hidden Markov Models

## Abstract

Behavioural sequences are often assumed to be generated by latent cognitive states, such as task engagement, exploration and lapses of attention, that switch over time. Discrete-state models such as hidden Markov models (HMMs) are the standard tool for recovering them, whereas variational autoencoders (VAEs) offer a continuous alternative that also permits "what-if" manipulations of the learned representation. This study tests, in a fully simulated setting where the ground truth is known, whether a windowed GRU-VAE recovers hidden states better than an HMM baseline and whether interventions on its latent space decode to behaviour consistent with the simulator's state-specific distributions. I simulated 200 subjects x 300 trials from a three-state (engaged, exploratory, lapsed) Markov process emitting binary correctness and log-RT, split by subject (70/15/15), and evaluated window-level recovery on held-out subjects. K-means on the VAE latents (validation-selected beta = 0.1) reached a test adjusted Rand index (ARI) of 0.30 (0.30 +/- 0.01 across five model seeds), exceeding the HMM specified in the study protocol (ARI 0.16), whose Gaussian emissions are mismatched to binary correctness, but falling well short of a correctly specified Bernoulli-Gaussian HMM (ARI 0.47). The protocol's loss function collapsed completely at beta >= 1. Setting the lapsed-associated latent dimension to the lapsed-window mean rather than the engaged-window mean produced decoded behaviour in the expected direction (longer RTs, lower correctness) but recovered only about 35-42% of the simulator's true effect. I conclude that the VAE captures state-related structure but that, for a process that is discrete by construction, a discrete-state model with the right emission distributions is the more appropriate and more accurate tool. Latent interventions are model-based counterfactuals, not causal inferences.

**Keywords:** latent cognitive states; variational autoencoder; hidden Markov model; posterior collapse; counterfactual simulation; reaction time

## 1. Background

### 1.1 Latent states in behaviour

Trial-by-trial choices and reaction times (RTs) fluctuate in ways that a single stationary decision process does not explain. A common account is that an observer moves among a small number of latent states, for example an engaged state with fast, accurate responding, an exploratory state with slower and less accurate responding, and a lapsed state in which responding approaches chance with long and variable RTs. Switching between discrete strategies has been documented with HMM-type models in perceptual decision-making (Ashwood et al., 2022), and HMMs are the conventional method for inferring such sequences (Rabiner, 1989). Because real latent states are never observed, the accuracy of any method can only be judged in simulation, where the generating states are known.

### 1.2 Generative deep models as an alternative

A VAE (Kingma & Welling, 2014) learns an encoder that maps data to a distribution over a continuous latent variable and a decoder that maps latent values back to data, trained by maximising the evidence lower bound (ELBO), a reconstruction term plus a Kullback-Leibler (KL) penalty toward a prior. The beta-VAE variant weights the KL term by beta to trade reconstruction fidelity against latent regularisation (Higgins et al., 2017). Two properties make VAEs attractive for behavioural data: the latent space is continuous and so can in principle express graded or mixed states, and the decoder permits manipulating the latent code and inspecting the behaviour it generates. Their characteristic failure mode is posterior collapse, in which the approximate posterior matches the prior and the latent carries no information about the input (Bowman et al., 2016).

### 1.3 Aims and research questions

The aims of this project were to test, using simulated sequences with known hidden states:

1. **RQ1.** Does a VAE's learned latent representation recover the simulator's hidden states better than an HMM baseline?
2. **RQ2.** Do interventions on the VAE's latent dimensions produce decoded choice/RT patterns consistent with the simulator's own ground-truth state manipulations?

"Counterfactual" is used here in the model-based sense only: it describes what the trained decoder produces under a different latent value. It is explicitly not causal inference about real cognition.

## 2. Methodology

All code, configuration (`config.yaml`, seed 2026) and tests are in the accompanying repository. Software: Python 3.13, PyTorch 2.12, hmmlearn 0.3.3, scikit-learn 1.7, SciPy 1.16.

### 2.1 Simulator

For each of 200 subjects, a hidden state sequence of 300 trials was drawn from a first-order Markov chain over three states (engaged, exploratory, lapsed), starting in the engaged state, with transition matrix (rows: current state)

| From \ To | Engaged | Exploratory | Lapsed |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Engaged | 0.90 | 0.07 | 0.03 |
| Exploratory | 0.10 | 0.80 | 0.10 |
| Lapsed | 0.10 | 0.20 | 0.70 |

Each trial emitted a binary correctness value and a log-RT with state-specific parameters:

| State | P(correct) | log-RT mean | log-RT SD |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Engaged | 0.85 | 0.20 | 0.25 |
| Exploratory | 0.65 | 0.40 | 0.30 |
| Lapsed | 0.50 | 0.90 | 0.45 |

The implied stationary distribution is approximately 0.57 / 0.34 / 0.10 (engaged / exploratory / lapsed). Seven unit tests verify that empirical state proportions and transition frequencies match their expected values, that lapsed trials are slower than engaged trials, trial counts, seed reproducibility, window shape and label correctness, and the subject-level split with training-only standardisation.

### 2.2 Windowing, labels and data split

Each subject's sequence was cut into 30 non-overlapping windows of 10 trials; each window is a 10 x 2 matrix of [correct, log-RT]. A window's true label is its majority hidden state. Subjects (not trials or windows) were split 70/15/15 into 140 training, 30 validation and 30 test subjects, so that no subject contributes to more than one set. Log-RT was standardised with the training-set mean and SD only; correctness was left as 0/1. The test set was used only for final evaluation, and all model selection used validation data.

Windows are frequently mixed: in the test set 73% of windows contain more than one hidden state, and the label distribution is imbalanced (57% engaged, 34% exploratory, 10% lapsed). This places a ceiling on the recovery any method can achieve.

### 2.3 HMM baselines

Two three-state HMMs were fit on the training subjects' trial sequences (one sequence per subject, windows concatenated), with the best of five initialisations chosen by training log-likelihood. Decoded trial states were reduced to window labels by majority vote, mirroring the definition of the true label.

- **Gaussian HMM (protocol baseline).** `hmmlearn.GaussianHMM` with full covariance, treating correctness as a numeric feature, as the project plan prescribes.
- **Bernoulli-Gaussian HMM (added).** A custom Baum-Welch implementation with Bernoulli emissions for correctness and Gaussian emissions for log-RT, with posterior-marginal decoding. This was added because the Gaussian model's likelihood is dominated by the binary feature (Section 3.1), making it an unrepresentatively weak baseline.

### 2.4 VAE

The VAE took a 10 x 2 window as input. A single-layer GRU encoder (hidden size 32) fed linear heads for the mean and log-variance of a 4-dimensional Gaussian latent; a GRU decoder (hidden size 32), initialised from the latent, produced a 10 x 2 reconstruction. The objective was reconstruction mean-squared error plus beta times the KL divergence (summed over latent dimensions, averaged over the batch). Training used Adam (learning rate 0.001, batch size 128) for up to 100 epochs with early stopping after 10 epochs without validation improvement, on CPU, with all random seeds fixed. Beta was set to 0.1, 1 and 5.

Because element-averaged MSE makes the KL term large relative to reconstruction, a sensitivity variant with squared error summed over the window (equivalent to a unit-variance Gaussian log-likelihood) was trained at the same three betas. This gives six VAE runs. The primary model was chosen as the run with the highest validation K-means ARI among runs retaining at least one active latent dimension (KL >= 0.01 nats); fully collapsed runs were excluded from this choice.

### 2.5 Evaluation

**State recovery (RQ1).** For the VAE, K-means (k = 3, 10 initialisations) was applied to posterior-mean latents of the held-out windows. For the HMMs, inferred window labels were used directly. Because cluster and state labels are arbitrary, labels were aligned to the true labels with Hungarian matching before label-based metrics. Reported metrics: ARI, normalised mutual information (NMI), macro-F1 after matching, and the mean absolute error (MAE) between the window-to-window transition matrix of the matched predicted labels and that of the true window labels. A negative control permuted the true labels (100 permutations). As a complement, a logistic-regression probe (z-scored features) mapped latents to true labels (trained on training subjects, evaluated on test subjects); this shows whether state information is present in the latents but is not an unsupervised recovery result.

**Posterior-collapse diagnostics.** For each latent dimension: mean posterior variance (versus the prior variance of 1), variance of the posterior means across windows, per-dimension KL, and the accuracy of a single-dimension probe relative to the majority-class rate. A dimension was flagged as collapsed if its KL was below 0.01 nats and its probe did not exceed the majority rate by more than 2 percentage points.

**Counterfactual intervention (RQ2).** For the primary model, the latent dimension with the largest standardised lapsed-versus-engaged mean difference in the training windows was selected. For every test window the encoder mean was computed, that dimension was set to the mean value among training windows with a true lapsed label (and, as a comparison condition, the engaged label), and the result was decoded and returned to the original log-RT scale. An additional condition replaced the whole latent vector by the state mean. Decoded windows were compared with 5,000 windows per state generated by the simulator with the hidden state clamped, on mean correctness, mean log-RT, within-window RT SD, the probabilities of a correct response given a previous correct or error response (with decoded correctness sampled as Bernoulli from the decoded probability), and the Wasserstein-1 distance between pooled RT distributions. Success was defined directionally: lapsed-direction decoding should give longer RTs and lower correctness than engaged-direction decoding.

**Success criteria (specified before running the analyses).** (i) *Primary:* the VAE's test ARI exceeds the HMM baseline's. (ii) *Secondary:* at least one latent dimension separates lapsed from engaged windows. (iii) *Counterfactual:* an intervention toward the lapsed-state region yields longer decoded RTs and lower decoded correctness than an intervention toward the engaged-state region. (iv) *Interpretive:* if the HMM outperforms the VAE, this is reported as evidence that a discrete-state model is more appropriate for this simulator, not as a failure. Criterion (i) was written with the project plan's HMM (`hmmlearn.GaussianHMM`, Day 2 of the plan) in mind; the Bernoulli-Gaussian HMM was added afterwards (Section 2.3).

## 3. Results

### 3.1 State recovery on held-out subjects

**Table 1.** Window-level state recovery on the 30 test subjects (900 windows). The shuffled-label control gave ARI 0.001, NMI 0.002 and macro-F1 0.34.

| Model | Val. ARI | Test ARI | Test NMI | Test macro-F1 | Transition MAE |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| HMM, Gaussian (protocol) | 0.144 | 0.158 | 0.105 | 0.372 | 0.247 |
| HMM, Bernoulli-Gaussian | 0.420 | 0.471 | 0.357 | 0.749 | 0.041 |
| VAE, MSE-mean loss, beta = 0.1 (primary) | 0.283 | 0.301 | 0.217 | 0.512 | 0.153 |
| VAE, MSE-mean loss, beta = 1 | 0.270 | 0.268 | 0.254 | 0.645 | 0.068 |
| VAE, MSE-mean loss, beta = 5 | 0.073 | 0.053 | 0.053 | 0.418 | 0.107 |
| VAE, summed loss, beta = 0.1 | 0.161 | 0.127 | 0.123 | 0.439 | 0.114 |
| VAE, summed loss, beta = 1 | 0.244 | 0.249 | 0.174 | 0.526 | 0.114 |
| VAE, summed loss, beta = 5 | 0.293 | 0.316 | 0.285 | 0.656 | 0.077 |

*Gaussian HMM.* This model did not recover cognitive states. Its three components corresponded to correct/fast, incorrect/fast and an outlier-RT component (state means in standardised space such as (0, 0.29), (1, -0.11) and (1, 6.1) for [correct, log-RT]), and its recovered transition matrix was unlike the generating matrix (trial-level MAE 0.37; trial-state accuracy 0.56). Increasing the covariance regularisation did not change this behaviour. This is a consequence of modelling a binary variable as Gaussian: variance on the binary dimension can shrink toward zero within a component, so the likelihood rewards separating correct from incorrect trials rather than separating cognitive states.

*Bernoulli-Gaussian HMM.* With the appropriate emission model, the HMM recovered the generating parameters closely: state-wise P(correct) of 0.86 / 0.66 / 0.50 (true 0.85 / 0.65 / 0.50) and a transition matrix within a mean absolute error of 0.04 of the true matrix (trial-state accuracy 0.78). It achieved the best window-level recovery (ARI 0.47).

*VAE.* The validation-selected VAE (MSE-mean loss, beta = 0.1; early-stopped at epoch 44) achieved test ARI 0.30, above the Gaussian HMM but below the Bernoulli-Gaussian HMM. Re-training this configuration under four additional model seeds (same dataset) gave a test ARI of 0.305 +/- 0.010 (range 0.298-0.319), so the VAE result is stable with respect to initialisation. A logistic probe on its latents reached 0.73 accuracy and 0.67 macro-F1 on test subjects, against a 0.57 majority-class accuracy, showing that state information is present in the latents.

### 3.2 Posterior collapse

**Table 2.** Per-dimension diagnostics of the primary VAE (beta = 0.1, test windows).

| Dimension | Mean posterior variance | KL (nats) | Single-dimension probe accuracy | Flagged collapsed |
|---|---:|---:|---:|:---:|
| z0 | 1.00 | 0.000 | 0.587 | yes |
| z1 | 0.42 | 0.409 | 0.719 | no |
| z2 | 0.89 | 0.059 | 0.568 | no |
| z3 | 0.85 | 0.086 | 0.568 | no |

Only z1 is strongly informative; z2 and z3 have non-zero KL but carry little single-dimension state information (probe accuracy at the majority rate). Under the protocol's loss, beta = 1 and beta = 5 collapsed completely (total KL about 2e-5 and 1e-5 nats, posterior variance about 1 in all dimensions, best validation epoch 11). With the summed reconstruction loss, beta = 0.1 and 1 kept four and three active dimensions respectively, but beta = 5 collapsed (total KL 2.4e-4 nats).

A caution on interpretation: collapse is a continuum rather than a binary property. Several runs with total KL near zero still gave above-chance z-scored probe accuracy (e.g. 0.72 for MSE-mean beta = 1; 0.74 for summed beta = 5) and non-trivial K-means ARI (0.27 and 0.32), because K-means is invariant to the scale of the latent and a linear probe can exploit a signal of tiny magnitude. These ARIs therefore do not indicate a healthily used latent space, and the primary model was selected only among runs with at least one active dimension.

### 3.3 Counterfactual latent interventions

The latent dimension most associated with the lapsed state was z1 (standardised lapsed-minus-engaged difference 3.2 SD; state means -0.43 for engaged and 1.22 for lapsed).

**Table 3.** Decoded behaviour after intervention versus two references: the simulator with the hidden state clamped (pure states) and the observed test windows grouped by their majority-state label (which contain mixed windows).

| Condition | Mean correctness | Mean log-RT | Within-window RT SD |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| VAE, no intervention | 0.734 | 0.350 | 0.040 |
| VAE, z1 -> engaged mean | 0.763 | 0.285 | 0.021 |
| VAE, z1 -> lapsed mean | 0.639 | 0.582 | 0.081 |
| VAE, all z -> engaged mean | 0.763 | 0.282 | 0.009 |
| VAE, all z -> lapsed mean | 0.640 | 0.579 | 0.053 |
| Simulator, engaged | 0.852 | 0.200 | 0.242 |
| Simulator, exploratory | 0.652 | 0.400 | 0.291 |
| Simulator, lapsed | 0.498 | 0.901 | 0.437 |
| Observed test windows, true label engaged | 0.802 | 0.271 | 0.307 |
| Observed test windows, true label lapsed | 0.569 | 0.676 | 0.464 |

The intervention met the directional criterion: moving z1 from the engaged to the lapsed mean increased decoded mean log-RT by 0.30 (simulator: 0.70) and reduced decoded correctness by 0.12 (simulator: 0.35), i.e. 42% and 35% of the clamped-state simulator's effects. Against the more like-for-like reference, observed test windows labelled lapsed versus engaged (RT difference 0.41, correctness difference 0.23), the decoded shifts are about 73% and 53% of the observed differences. Because the latent state means were estimated from such majority-labelled, partly mixed windows, the observed-window contrast is the fairer benchmark, and the clamped-state contrast is a stricter upper bound. The Wasserstein distance between decoded and simulator RT distributions was 0.39 for the lapsed condition and 0.19 for the engaged condition (0.58 and 0.15 for unmodified decoding compared with lapsed and engaged ground truth, respectively). Intervening on all latent dimensions gave almost the same means as intervening on z1 alone, consistent with z1 carrying most of the state-related signal. Decoded within-window RT variability (0.02-0.08) was an order of magnitude below the simulator's (0.24-0.44), and sampled correctness sequences showed no serial dependence (P(correct | correct) about equal to P(correct | error)), as in the simulator. The bootstrap intervals computed over test windows are extremely narrow (about +/- 0.0003) but reflect the near-determinism of the decoder rather than uncertainty about the effect.

| Criterion | Outcome |
|---|---|

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Does the VAE recover hidden states better than an HMM?

The answer depends on which HMM is the comparator, and this dependency is the main finding. Against the HMM specified in the protocol the VAE is clearly better, but that comparison is uninformative because the Gaussian HMM fails for a reason unrelated to the question: it treats binary correctness as continuous and so partitions trials by outcome rather than by state. Against an HMM whose emission model matches the data-generating process, the VAE is clearly worse (ARI 0.30 vs 0.47; macro-F1 0.51 vs 0.75; transition MAE 0.15 vs 0.04). This outcome is not surprising: the simulator is a discrete-state Markov process, which is exactly the model class the HMM assumes, and the HMM exploits temporal structure across windows that the VAE, which encodes each window independently, cannot. The VAE has no mechanism that favours clustered latents, and 73% of windows contain a mixture of states, which a single continuous code must represent as an intermediate value. These results are consistent with the interpretive criterion set in advance: when a discrete-state model outperforms the VAE, a discrete-state model is more appropriate for a simulator that is itself discrete. They do not show that VAEs are unsuitable for behavioural data in general. The comparison would shift with real data that include graded or continuously varying internal variables, which the HMM would represent poorly.

Evidence that the VAE does learn state-related structure is nonetheless substantial: its ARI is far above the shuffled control, a linear probe recovers state with 0.73 accuracy against a 0.57 baseline, and one latent dimension (z1) separates lapsed from engaged windows by more than three standard deviations. The result was stable across model seeds.

The geometry of the latent space helps explain the ceiling on clustering. A UMAP projection of the test latents (Figure 3) shows engaged, exploratory and lapsed windows arranged along a continuous left-to-right gradient, with overlapping rather than separated groups: the VAE has learned an ordered "severity" axis (consistent with z1 carrying most of the signal) instead of three discrete clusters. K-means with k = 3 must cut this continuum at arbitrary points, which limits ARI even though the latent is informative, whereas an HMM imposes discreteness by construction. The same axis is what makes graded interventions on z1 meaningful.

### 4.2 Posterior collapse and the training objective

The protocol's loss, element-averaged MSE plus beta x summed KL, strongly favours collapse. Reconstruction error is a mean over 20 numbers per window with values around 1, whereas the KL is summed over four latent dimensions, so even beta = 1 makes it cheaper to discard the latent than to encode it, and the model learned to output near-constant reconstructions (validation reconstruction MSE about 0.58). Only beta = 0.1 retained informative dimensions. Rescaling the reconstruction term (summed loss) retained more active dimensions at low beta and gave the best ARI at beta = 5 (0.32), but that run is itself near-collapsed by the KL criterion, which illustrates why ARI should be reported together with KL and probe diagnostics rather than alone. The diagnostics also showed that collapse is graded: informative signal can survive at a very small scale, and a scale-invariant clustering algorithm will find it.

### 4.3 Interpreting the counterfactual results

The intervention produced a coherent directional effect: shifting a single latent dimension toward its lapsed-window mean made decoded behaviour slower and less accurate, in the direction and roughly the order of magnitude of the simulator's lapsed-versus-engaged difference. This shows that the VAE's latent geometry is aligned with the simulator's state structure along that dimension. It does not show that the model has recovered the mechanism that generated the data, nor that interventions on the latent would identify causal effects in real cognition. The decoder is a regression to the conditional mean of the window, so (i) effects are attenuated (35-42% of the clamped-state shift, but 53-73% of the difference observed between majority-lapsed and majority-engaged test windows), partly because a window labelled "lapsed" is often a mixture of states, so its latent mean is less extreme than a pure lapsed state and the clamped-state comparison is deliberately strict; (ii) decoded RT variability is largely lost; and (iii) trial-to-trial dependence is not represented. A decoder with a probabilistic output (for example Bernoulli and Gaussian likelihoods for the two observation types) would be a more faithful test of whether the decoded distribution, rather than just its mean, matches the simulator.

### 4.3.1 Counterfactual versus causal

The latent intervention asks what the trained model would decode under a different latent value. The clamped-state simulator provides a ground truth for the *simulator's* state manipulation, which is why the comparison is meaningful here. In real data no such ground truth exists, so the same procedure would yield descriptive model behaviour only, not a causal claim about the underlying cognitive process.

### 4.4 Limitations

1. **Single simulated dataset.** Seed robustness was assessed for VAE initialisation and training order only; variation across newly simulated datasets was not examined, so differences of a few ARI points between runs should not be over-interpreted. The VAE-versus-HMM conclusion rests on a gap (0.30 vs 0.47) that is much larger than the model-seed variation (SD 0.01), but no confidence interval across datasets is available.
2. **A simulator that favours the HMM.** The generating process is a first-order Markov chain with conditionally independent emissions, which is the HMM's own assumption. The result may not transfer to data with graded states, non-Markov dynamics or state-dependent temporal structure.
3. **Window-level label ambiguity.** Majority-vote labels over mixed windows (73% of test windows) and class imbalance (10% lapsed) bound all recovery metrics, and ARI penalises predictions for ambiguous windows that are not wrong in a meaningful sense.
4. **Asymmetric information.** HMMs were fit on full trial sequences and used temporal context across windows, whereas the VAE encoded each window independently; the comparison therefore conflates model class and context. An HMM restricted to single windows, or a VAE with a sequence-level latent, would isolate the model class more cleanly.
5. **Added and specified baselines.** The protocol HMM is a weak comparator, and the Bernoulli-Gaussian HMM was introduced after seeing the former fail. It is a principled correction (correct emission model), but it is an analytic choice made in light of the results.
6. **Model selection.** The primary VAE was selected on validation ARI, which uses validation labels; the model is nonetheless unsupervised in training and the test labels were untouched. The six-run grid was small and the architecture (hidden size 32, 4-d latent) was not tuned.
7. **Deterministic decoder and counterfactual metrics.** The MSE decoder yields conditional means, understating variability and attenuating effects; bootstrap intervals over windows understate uncertainty; and the correctness-sequence statistic relies on Bernoulli sampling of the decoded probability, so it contains no information about serial dependence learned by the model.
8. **Collapse diagnostics.** The collapse thresholds (KL < 0.01 nats; probe within 2 points of the majority rate) are heuristic, and collapse is graded rather than binary.
9. **No real data.** Optional extensions to real behavioural data or to a second simulated test bed (a reversal-learning simulator) were not run. Results are simulation-only and make no claims about real cognition.

### 4.5 Conclusion

In a controlled simulation, a windowed GRU-VAE learned a latent space that carries state information and supports directionally correct latent interventions, but it recovered the hidden states less accurately than an HMM with matched emission distributions, and the loss specified in the protocol collapsed at beta >= 1. For a process that is discrete by construction, the discrete-state model is the better choice; the principal value of the VAE lies in settings where states may be graded or unknown in form, and where its decoder can be used for model-based what-if exploration, provided its collapse behaviour and its attenuated, mean-regressed decoding are monitored and reported.

## Reproducibility and code/data availability

All data are simulated; no human or animal data were used. The full pipeline (simulator, windowing, HMM baselines, VAE training, evaluation, counterfactuals, figures) is in the repository https://github.com/karenccl/vae-latent-cognitive-states. Parameters are fixed in `config.yaml` (seed 2026) and software versions are pinned in `environment.yml`. Seven unit tests (`python -m pytest`) cover the simulator, windowing and subject-level split. The scripts in `src/` regenerate every table and figure; the run order is given in the README, and the numerical results are stored in `results/` (including `summary.csv`). Model checkpoints and latent vectors are in `outputs/`. Re-running the evaluation and counterfactual scripts reproduced the stored results exactly. Training was on CPU and takes a few minutes per model.

## Appendix A: Summary

I built a simulation study testing whether a variational autoencoder (VAE) can recover hidden cognitive states (engaged, exploratory, lapsed) from choice and reaction-time sequences, compared with hidden Markov models (HMMs), using held-out simulated subjects. The VAE recovered states above the HMM specified in the project plan (ARI 0.30 vs 0.16) but below an HMM with correctly matched emission distributions (0.47), and posterior collapse occurred at higher regularisation strengths. Latent interventions shifted decoded behaviour in the expected direction (slower, less accurate) at 35-73% of the true effect depending on the reference. The project demonstrates generative modelling, baseline-controlled evaluation, collapse diagnostics and careful distinction between model-based counterfactuals and causal inference, in reproducible Python code.
